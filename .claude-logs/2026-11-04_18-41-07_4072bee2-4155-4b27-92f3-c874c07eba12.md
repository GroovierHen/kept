## My recommendation: a "Comeback" feature for the day after a miss

Users don't usually leave on a bad day. They leave after a miss, when they have to decide whether to come back. Kept already avoids punishing a miss: an unreported day closes as Unreported, and two Noes make the next promise smaller. But nothing in the app treats coming back as something worth noticing, and the ledger still shows a "Slipped" count.

The feature has three parts:

1. **A kept-days streak that survives one missed day.** One Unreported or No day doesn't break it, so a single miss never shows as a broken run.
2. **Coming back counts as a win.** The first check-in after a miss is called out on Today and in the Ledger ("Back on track"). The slip itself isn't the headline.
3. **A fresh start after a longer gap.** After several days away, the next promise starts at small, timed to the next Monday.

It needs no new AI call, so it fits the walkthrough's rule that AI is used in only two places.

## The evidence behind each part

| Finding | Source | What it means for Kept |
|---|---|---|
| Of 54 programs tested on about 61,000 gym members, the best one gave small rewards for **returning after a missed workout**. 45% of the programs raised weekly visits, by 9% to 27%. | Milkman et al., *Nature* 2021 | Reward the return, not just the streak |
| Missing one day didn't meaningfully slow habit formation. Repeated misses lowered the final habit strength over time. | Lally et al., UCL 2009/2010 | The risk is the second miss, so the comeback is the moment to act |
| A log showing an **intact** streak led to more later engagement than one showing a broken streak. The effect was weaker when users could **repair** the streak. Messages pointing out a failure "typically doesn't work." | Silverman & Barasch, *J. Consumer Research* 2023 | Let the streak survive a miss, and don't highlight slips |
| Duolingo's Weekend Amulet, which protects the streak over a weekend: users were 4% more likely to return a week later and 5% less likely to lose their streak. Streak Wager raised day-7 retention by 14%. | Duolingo blog | Streak protection works at scale |
| Users on a 7-day streak were 2.4× more likely to come back the next day. This is observational, not an experiment. | Duolingo blog | |
| People are more likely to start working toward goals after "fresh start" dates such as a new week or month. | Dai, Milkman & Riis, *Management Science* 2014 | Time the fresh start to Monday |
| Monitoring your progress helps you reach goals (d = 0.40 across 138 trials), and the effect is larger when progress is recorded or reported. | Harkin et al., *Psychological Bulletin* 2016 | The Ledger is the right place to show it |
| In HeartSteps, the boost from a phone prompt shrank over 6 weeks. | Klasnja et al. 2019 | Sending more reminders isn't the answer |

For context, the reports I found put day-30 retention for typical health and fitness apps at roughly 3–8%. The figures vary by tracker and year.

## Limits of this evidence

- **None of these studies tested Kept.** They cover gyms, language learning and walking. They show the idea is well supported, not that it will work for Kept.
- **Some exact numbers come from summaries.** I confirmed the megastudy headline from its abstract. The often-quoted "27% increase" for the missed-workout reward appears only in press coverage, and I couldn't check it against the paper.
- **I didn't look at Kept's own data.** Before building, check where Kept's testers actually stop checking in. If they drop off during setup rather than after a miss, the right feature is different.
- **Timing:** the feature freeze was Nov 3 and the demo is Nov 5, so this is work for after the demo.

**Runner-up idea:** a 30-second weekly review every Monday, based on the Harkin and fresh-start findings. It has weaker evidence specifically for bringing users back.

Yes. The streak is the first part of Comeback. It's built to survive a single miss, because the research says one missed day is harmless and that a log showing a broken streak makes people quit.

Below is how it would work using Kept's existing answers (Yes, Partly, No, Unreported). The specific rules are my proposal; next to each I've noted the finding it follows.

## The rules

| Rule | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. A Yes or Partly adds a day** | The streak goes up by 1. | Kept already counts Partly as half-kept, and treating effort as success keeps people going. Counting Partly is my design choice. |
| **2. One miss doesn't break it** | A single No or Unreported day doesn't add a day, but the streak survives. The app says "Your streak is safe. Report back tomorrow to keep it." | Lally: one missed day didn't slow habit formation. Barasch: the harm from a broken streak was weaker when it could be repaired. |
| **3. Two misses in a row end it** | The streak ends. | Lally: repeated misses do add up. This also matches Kept's rule that two Noes shrink the promise. |
| **4. Protection comes back after 3 kept days in a row** | After one miss is used, it becomes available again after three Yes or Partly days in a row. | Without this, someone could alternate Yes and No forever and never break the streak. Three days matches Kept's "three Yeses and the promise grows" rule. |
| **5. A broken streak is never the headline** | No "You lost your 12-day streak." The ledger shows the **best streak** and starts a Comeback. | Barasch: messages pointing out a broken streak "typically doesn't work." |

## What happens after the streak breaks (the Comeback part)

- **The first check-in back is the win.** Today shows "You're back. Day 1 of your next streak." The ledger records it as a comeback, not as a list of slips. *(Megastudy: the best of 54 programs rewarded returning after a missed workout.)*
- **The next promise starts small**, so coming back is easy.
- **After a long gap** (say a week away), Kept offers a fresh start on the next Monday. *(Fresh start effect.)*

## Sara's example

| Day | Report | Streak | Screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Yes | 1 | |
| 3 | Yes | 2 | |
| 4 | Yes | 3 | Protection ready |
| 5 | Unreported | 3 | "Your streak is safe" (protection used) |
| 6 | Partly | 4 | |
| 7 | No | 4 | Protection not back yet (only 1 kept day since day 5)… |
| 8 | No | ends | …so day 7 + day 8 = two misses in a row |
| 9 | Yes | 1 | "You're back. Day 1 of your next streak." Best streak: 4 |

Wait, that table breaks the streak on two misses, but day 7 alone isn't two in a row. Under rule 2 the streak would survive day 7 only if protection were available. Since protection wasn't refilled (rule 4), day 7 ends it. So the streak actually ends on **day 7**, and day 8 is just a second missed day. The point stands: one miss is forgiven when protection is ready, and a second miss before three kept days in a row ends the streak.

## Where it shows

- **Today screen:** the streak number and whether protection is ready.
- **Ledger:** current streak, best streak, and comebacks.
- **Results page:** could add "people who came back after a miss" across testers. That's a stronger retention number for judges than streak length.

## Two decisions for you

1. **Should Partly keep the streak alive?** I'd say yes, but you could count only Yes.
2. **Should an Unreported day and a No count the same?** For promise sizing, Kept ignores Unreported days. For the streak I'd count both as a miss, otherwise someone could just never report and keep their streak forever.

There are two reasons. One protects the judges' numbers, the other protects real testers.

**1. Your test account would show up on the public Results page.**
The Results page counts every account except the demo account and ones marked "not counted". It's your proof for the judges: real testers, real check-ins. A test account you jump through time would add made-up numbers there:
- a sign-up that isn't a tester,
- check-ins on days that never happened,
- a Comeback you made by skipping days on purpose.

The walkthrough promises the judges that "the demo account and our own accounts aren't counted", so test accounts need excluding to keep that promise.

**2. It's the safety catch for jumping time.**
The jump command moves an account's data back in time on the production database. If you mistype an email and it matches a real tester, you'd scramble their streak and their ledger. Making the jump refuse any account still counted means it only ever works on accounts you've deliberately marked as test accounts. A real tester is always counted, so the command can't touch them.

**Why it isn't automatic inside the jump:** if the jump excluded the account itself, a mistyped email would silently remove a real tester from the Results page and then move their data. Keeping it a separate, deliberate step means a mistake fails safely instead of quietly doing damage.

You only run `exclude` once per test account, right after signing up. Every jump after that is just the one command.
